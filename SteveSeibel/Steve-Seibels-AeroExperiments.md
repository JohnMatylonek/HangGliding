# Steve Seibel’s AeroExperiments: Investigating the Hidden Relationship Between Yaw, Sideslip, and Roll

*An archival overview by John Matylonek*

## A Pilot Investigating What Pilots Actually See and Feel

Hang-glider pilots learn early that shifting their weight rolls the glider and that banking initiates a turn. That practical description is useful, but it leaves a complicated interval unexplained. Between the pilot’s roll input and the establishment of a steady turn, the glider may roll, yaw, slip, and change direction at different rates. The nose may briefly move in a direction that does not accurately reveal the changing flight path.

Steve Seibel’s **AeroExperiments** work concentrated on this easily overlooked interval.

His writings did not treat yaw, roll, sideslip, and turning as interchangeable descriptions of a single motion. He repeatedly separated four questions:

1. Which direction is the aircraft’s nose pointing?
2. Which direction is the aircraft actually traveling through the airmass?
3. How is the aircraft rolling about its longitudinal axis?
4. How quickly is the flight path curving?

This distinction is the foundation of the archive. Much of the confusion surrounding turning flight arises when movement of the nose is assumed to be identical to movement of the aircraft through the air.

## An Experimental Approach to Familiar Assumptions

AeroExperiments combined geometric models, pilot observation, yaw strings, slip-skid indicators, controllable rudders, wingtip drag devices, and comparisons among hang gliders, sailplanes, conventional aircraft, and model wings.

The purpose was not simply to collect unusual flying demonstrations. Steve used experiments to isolate variables that ordinary flight combines. A pilot normally experiences roll, yaw, sideslip, and turn curvature together. By deliberately inducing yaw or observing the response to a sideways airflow, he sought to determine which motion caused which aerodynamic response.

This approach made AeroExperiments unusual. Rather than beginning with the pilot’s impression that “the nose went the wrong way” or “the glider did not want to turn,” Steve asked what the nose, flight path, relative wind, and wings were each doing at that moment.

His work was therefore both aerodynamic and perceptual. It investigated the aircraft, but also the limits of what a pilot can infer by watching the nose.

## The Central Insight: Sideways Airflow Couples Yaw to Roll

One of Steve’s clearest arguments concerns dihedral and anhedral.

A common informal explanation says that when an aircraft with dihedral or anhedral banks or slips, one wing somehow becomes “more horizontal,” gains more projected area, or develops a more vertical lift vector. Steve argued that this misses the central mechanism.

The important event is a **sideways component in the relative wind**.

When an aircraft moves sideways through the airmass—or when its nose yaws away from the direction in which the aircraft is actually traveling—the airflow approaches partly from one side. On a wing with dihedral or anhedral, that sideways airflow changes the effective angle of attack differently on the two sides of the wing. The unequal aerodynamic response produces a rolling moment.

This creates a coupling between yaw and roll:

- With effective **dihedral**, yaw or sideslip tends to create a roll in the same directional sense.
- With effective **anhedral**, yaw or sideslip tends to create a roll in the opposite directional sense.

The strength and even the direction of this coupling depend on the aircraft’s geometry, yaw stability, airspeed, billow, washout, sweep, and other aerodynamic features.

The conceptual point is simple even when the full dynamics are not: yaw affects roll because yaw can create sideways airflow across a wing whose geometry converts that airflow into unequal angles of attack.

## Why Billow and Washout Matter

A flex-wing hang glider cannot be understood as a rigid, flat swept wing.

The sail rises and twists under aerodynamic load. The trailing edge may rise in the mid-span region, producing billow, while washout changes the local angle and orientation of the outboard wing sections. Steve argued that these features contribute to the glider’s **net geometric anhedral**.

This matters because visible leading-edge geometry alone may give a misleading impression of the aircraft’s effective dihedral or anhedral. A glider can have a swept planform that would ordinarily encourage positive yaw–roll coupling, yet its billow and washout may produce enough effective anhedral to reverse that response under some conditions.

Variable geometry adds another dimension. Tightening the sail changes billow and twist, potentially changing both roll response and yaw–roll coupling. AeroExperiments treated these not as isolated design details but as parts of a connected three-dimensional geometry.

## Adverse Yaw and the Misleading Nose

During a roll input, the rising wing may experience increased drag and the aircraft’s nose may initially yaw opposite the intended turn. This is adverse yaw.

A pilot watching the nose can interpret that motion as evidence that the aircraft is not turning. Steve emphasized that the conclusion does not necessarily follow.

The flight path may already be curving toward the intended turn even while the nose briefly yaws the other way. The nose can point to one side of the actual flight path while both later rotate around the horizon in the turn direction. What the pilot sees is real, but its meaning depends on distinguishing **heading** from **direction of travel**.

Steve did not argue that adverse yaw is irrelevant. His 2007 article asks whether it strongly decreases turn rate in flex-wing hang gliders and considers several mechanisms by which it may have unfavorable effects. His conclusion is cautious. Because a hang glider lacks a large fuselage and fixed vertical tail, a sideways airflow may create less opposing aerodynamic side force than it would in a conventional aircraft. Adverse yaw may therefore look more obstructive from the pilot’s viewpoint than its actual effect on flight-path curvature would suggest.

At the same time, adverse yaw can create other aerodynamic penalties, including unfavorable rolling moments associated with changing wingtip speeds. Steve’s work resists replacing one oversimplification with another.

## The Importance of the Transitional Phase

The most distinctive contribution of AeroExperiments may be its attention to the transition before yaw, roll, bank, and flight-path curvature settle into their more familiar relationship.

Textbook descriptions often begin with a stable initial condition and end with a stabilized turn. Pilot instruction also tends to emphasize the control input and desired result. The intervening motion is harder to describe because several rotations and aerodynamic responses are developing simultaneously.

Yet that interval is precisely where pilots feel lag, hesitation, adverse yaw, changing bar pressure, or a nose that seems to “lie” about the direction of travel.

Steve’s work gives this interval analytical importance. It suggests that the handling qualities of a hang glider cannot be understood entirely from steady-state diagrams. Aircraft behavior during the transition may be central to what pilots experience as responsiveness, coordination, stability, or resistance to turn entry.

## Revision Was Part of the Work

The archive should not be read as a single finished textbook.

Some documents identify themselves as early editions. Steve anticipated adding photographs and revising parts of the presentation. In his longer theory compilation, he also described changes in his own thinking. In particular, he reconsidered an earlier assumption that most modern flex-wing hang gliders would show positive coupling between yaw and roll because of sweep. Later observations led him to emphasize that billow, washout, and effective anhedral could produce negative coupling at many airspeeds.

That willingness to revise is not a weakness of the archive. It reveals the experimental character of the project. AeroExperiments records the development of an investigation: observations generated hypotheses, physical models clarified geometry, flight experiments tested expectations, and conclusions changed when the accumulated evidence no longer supported the earlier view.

Readers should therefore distinguish among:

- direct observations;
- geometric demonstrations;
- proposed aerodynamic explanations;
- conclusions Steve regarded as well supported;
- tentative claims awaiting further testing;
- positions he later revised.

## Why Preserve AeroExperiments?

AeroExperiments deserves preservation for several reasons.

First, it documents an unusually sustained attempt by an experienced pilot to investigate the mechanics underlying pilot perception. The work connects practical flying experience with geometric and aerodynamic reasoning.

Second, it preserves questions that remain educationally valuable even when individual claims invite criticism or refinement. How do we distinguish heading from flight path? What actually creates dihedral effect? How does flexible-wing geometry change yaw–roll coupling? What can a pilot infer from the apparent movement of the nose?

Third, the archive demonstrates a civic virtue within technical culture: claims about aircraft behavior should remain open to observation, replication, disagreement, and correction. Authority alone cannot settle an aerodynamic question. Neither can intuition. A useful explanation must survive contact with geometry, flight behavior, and evidence.

Finally, the work captures a form of intellectual accomplishment that is easily lost. Much practical aviation knowledge has lived on personal websites, in club discussions, in magazine articles, and in the memories of pilots. When those websites disappear, the experimental record and the questions behind it can disappear as well.

Preserving AeroExperiments does not require treating every statement as final. It means preserving the work carefully enough that future pilots, instructors, designers, and researchers can understand what Steve observed, what he proposed, how his thinking changed, and what remains worth testing.
